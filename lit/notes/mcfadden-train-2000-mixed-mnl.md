# McFadden & Train (2000) — Mixed MNL Models for Discrete Response

**Full citation:** McFadden, Daniel and Kenneth Train (2000), "Mixed MNL Models for Discrete Response," *Journal of Applied Econometrics*, 15(5), 447–470.
**Type:** Journal article (theory + methods + application); the foundational mixed logit paper
**Length:** 24 journal pages (pp. 447–470; PDF has 25 pages incl. JSTOR cover — journal p. 447 = PDF p. 2)

## One-paragraph summary

The canonical theoretical justification for mixed (random-coefficients) multinomial logit. The paper (i) defines the MMNL model P_C(i|x,θ) = ∫ L_C(i; x, α) G(dα; θ) — an MNL kernel mixed over a distribution of coefficients; (ii) proves (Theorem 1) that under mild regularity conditions *any* discrete choice model derived from RUM can be approximated as closely as desired by an MMNL model, so mixed logit is a fully general platform, not just one more specification; (iii) develops practical estimation by Maximum Simulated Likelihood (MSLE) and Method of Simulated Moments (MSM), including unbiased smooth simulators, asymptotics (r·N^{-1/2} → ∞), Halton draws, the robust sandwich covariance estimator, and easily computed near-efficient instruments for MSM; (iv) derives Lagrange-multiplier specification tests for the presence of mixing (Theorem 2) and for omitted mixing in an estimated MMNL (Theorem 3) that require only simple artificial-variable regressions; and (v) applies the toolkit to the Brownstone–Train California alternative-fuel vehicle conjoint data (4,654 respondents), showing significant random effects and demonstrating that the artificial-variable test picks up mixing quickly and cheaply. When the mixing distribution has finite support, MMNL is a latent class model — so the framework covers that case too.

## Key contributions relevant to the book

- **The approximation theorem:** any RUM-consistent choice behavior ≈ MMNL. This is the theoretical license for the book's mixed-logit chapter and for using an MNL kernel to simulate/approximate MNP (interpretable "as simulation approximations using a MNL kernel," p. 451).
- **MSLE mechanics done right:** unbiased probability simulator, biased-but-consistent log/score simulators, fixed draws for stochastic equicontinuity, r vs. N rate condition, Halton sequences, and the warning that the "asymptotic" covariance underestimates variability for finite r — use the robust Γ^{-1}ΔΓ^{-1} sandwich.
- **MSM with practical instruments:** generalized-residual moment conditions and a second-order Taylor expansion trick giving cheap instruments with ~90%+ efficiency.
- **Specification tests students can actually run:** estimate plain MNL, construct z_ti = ½(x_ti − x_iC)², re-estimate with the z's, do a Wald/LR test — asymptotically an LM test of no mixing. No simulation needed for the diagnostic.
- **A complete worked application** with MNL vs. MMNL comparison tables, likelihood-ratio model selection, and asymptotic vs. robust standard errors at r = 250 and r = 50.

## Models & notation

- MMNL definition: P_C(i|x, θ) = ∫ L_C(i; x, α) G(dα; θ), with MNL kernel L_C(i; x, α) = e^{x_iα}/Σ_{j∈C} e^{x_jα}; x_i are 1×K vectors of functions of attributes and consumer characteristics; α is K×1 random; θ = deep parameters of mixing distribution G (eq. 1, p. 447). Finite-support G ⇒ latent class model (p. 447). Alternative-specific dummies in x_i ⇒ error-components interpretation (p. 447).
- History: introduced by Boyd–Mellman (1980) and Cardell–Dunbar (1980); antecedents in aggregating MNL over explanatory-variable distributions (Talvitie 1972; Westin 1974) (p. 448).
- RUM setup: resource allocation q = (g, l, z, ζ); observed consumer characteristics s, unobserved ς; preferences continuous; conditional indirect utility function given a discrete alternative, increasing in a−c, homogeneous degree zero and quasi-convex in (a−c, p, w) (eq. 2 and following, pp. 449–450). Unobservables represented as *uniformly distributed continuous random fields* via regular canonical representations (Lemmas 2–3) — e.g., Gaussian field X(t) = Λ(t)Φ^{-1}(ε(t)) (p. 449).
- No-ties condition: Jacobian rank condition guaranteeing zero probability of ties (p. 450).
- **Theorem 1 (p. 451):** for choice probabilities P*_C(i|z,s) generated by maximization of a canonical conditional indirect utility U*(z_j, s, ε_j, υ), and any η > 0, there exists x = x(z,s) and an MMNL model P_C(i|x,θ) within η of P*. Proof: Bernstein–Weierstrass polynomial approximation of U*, perturbed by scaled i.i.d. EV1 disturbances ν/k*² (pp. 465–466). Random coefficients α are polynomial transformations of the underlying uniform fields.
- Extensions: repeated/dynamic choices via products of MNL kernels mixed over G, with state dependence and serial correlation; Heckman initial-values caveat (p. 451).
- Related results: "mother logit" (McFadden 1984) — MNL in functions of all variables approximates any discrete choice model but is not guaranteed RUM-consistent; Dagsvik (1994) GEV approximation (p. 452).
- Identification caveats (p. 453): usual local/global identification issues; estimates can "blow up" (ψ → ∞) if the linear-in-α specification with i.i.d. EV1 noise is *exact* — rarely a practical problem; necessary and sufficient condition for additive i.i.d. EV1 components is a multivariate Laplace transform sign condition on F(−log t_1, ..., −log t_J) (p. 453; Lemma 4, pp. 464–465).
- Empirical parameterization: α = β + Λζ, β a K×1 mean vector, Λ a K×M factor-loading matrix (exclusions for identification), ζ ~ i.i.d. standard density f(ζ); θ' = (β', vec(Λ')') (Section 3, p. 453). Notation x_iC(ζ) = x_i − Σ_j x_j L_C(j; x, β+Λζ).

## Estimation details

- **Simulators (Section 3.1, pp. 453–454):** with Monte Carlo draws ζ_p, p = 1..r from f(ζ):
  - P^r_C(i|x,θ) = (1/r)Σ_p L_C(i; x, β+Λζ_p) (eq. 4) — positive, *unbiased*, smooth (continuously differentiable to all orders in θ).
  - Conditional expectations for derivatives simulated by E_{r|i} b(ζ) = {E_r b(ζ)·L_C(...)}/P^r_C (eq. 5) — importance-sampling interpretation; biased for finite r but consistent as r → ∞; log P^r simulator likewise biased but consistent. Unbiased accept–reject versions exist but lose smoothness.
  - **Stochastic equicontinuity:** keep draws ζ_p fixed as θ is adjusted during iteration (store draws or regenerate from fixed seeds) so simulators don't "chatter" (p. 454).
- **MSLE (Section 3.2, pp. 454–455):** maximize E_N log P^r_C(i|x,θ). Asymptotics (Hajivassiliou–McFadden 1997): if r·N^{-1/2} → ∞, MSLE is asymptotically equivalent to classical MLE; bias-free estimates in moderate samples likely need r considerably larger than N^{1/2}. Draws need not be independent across observations; Halton sequences give lower MSE than independent random draws (Train 1999) (p. 454).
  - Covariance estimation (eq. 6, p. 455): Γ_N(θ) = −E_N ∇_{θθ'} log P^r and Δ_N(θ) = E_N{∇_θ log P^r}{∇_θ log P^r}'. For finite r, Δ_N exceeds Γ_N due to simulation noise, and Δ_N^{-1} *underestimates* the estimator's covariance (can falsely suggest more draws reduce precision). **Recommendation: robust sandwich Γ_N^{-1} Δ_N Γ_N^{-1}** (quasi-ML form, Newey–McFadden) (p. 455).
- **MSM (Section 3.3, pp. 455–456):** generalized residual d_i − E_ζ L_C(i; x, β+Λζ) orthogonal to instruments W_i(x,θ) (eq. 7); replacing P_C with the *unbiased* simulator and using statistically independent simulators for instruments yields a consistent, asymptotically normal MSM estimator *without* requiring r → ∞ with N (McFadden 1989). Covariance Ψ_N^{-1}Σ_NΨ_N^{-1} (formulas p. 455). Efficiency approaches MLE as W_i approaches the score; simulated scores need large r.
  - **Cheap instruments (eq. 8, p. 456):** second-order Taylor expansion of the MNL kernel in ζ around 0 (Talvitie 1972): E_ζ L_C ≈ L_C(i;x,β)·{1 + ½tr(Λ'Q_iCΛ)}, Q_iC = x'_iC x_iC − Σ_j L_C(j;x,β) x'_jC x_jC; gradient gives W_i(x,θ)' = [x_iC, vec(Q_iCΛ')']. β can be set to simple MNL estimates. Monte Carlo evidence: MSM asymptotic efficiencies over 90%.
- **Specification testing (Section 4, pp. 456–458):**
  - **Theorem 2 (p. 456):** estimate simple MNL; construct artificial variables z_ti = ½(x_ti − x_iC)² with x_iC = Σ_j x_ij L_C(j; x, α̂) for selected components t; Wald or LR test of omitting the z's from an MNL with both x and z ≈ LM test of no mixing against MMNL with mixing in components t. df = number of linearly independent z's. Pivotal: null distribution doesn't depend on the mixing-distribution parameterization.
  - Monte Carlo (p. 457): nominal and actual sizes agree well (e.g., 5% nominal → 5.0% and 3.9% actual); power modest (8–52% depending on design) — heterogeneity is hard to distinguish from added EV noise, so use generous significance levels when screening (p. 458).
  - **Theorem 3 (p. 458, eq. 10):** test an *estimated* MMNL (base mixing f(ζ)) against additional mixing with up to T extra factors: construct simulated quantities v_i, w_i, y_ti from the base-model draws; regress 1 on (v_i, w_i, y_ti) over alternatives and observations; F-test of the y's ≈ LM test of λ = 0. Proofs use the √λ parameterization to avoid the identically-zero-score problem (pp. 466–468).
- **Application (Section 5, pp. 458–462):** Brownstone–Train California alternative-fuel vehicle conjoint; 4,654 respondents, 6 alternatives from 120 profiles (4 fuels × 5 sizes × 6 body types). Table I variable definitions (p. 459). Table II: Brownstone–Train MMNL with four random effects (non-EV, non-CNG, size, luggage space), MSLE with r = 250; asymptotic vs. robust SEs at r = 250 and r = 50 — asymptotic formula underestimates SEs by 10–20% at r = 250 (p. 460). Table III: base MNL (LL = −7391.83) and MNL + artificial variables (LL = −7356.61); LR test rejects no mixing; |T| > 1 flags candidates including two (operating cost, station availability) missed by the original selection procedure (pp. 460–461). Table IV: MMNL with six random effects, LL = −7358.93, significant mixing in all six; LR test favors it over Table II model; caveat — several factor combinations fit near-equally well, reflecting the inherent difficulty of identifying the factor structure of unobserved utility from discrete choices (p. 462).
- **Conclusions (p. 463):** MMNL + MSLE/MSM is "a flexible and computationally practical econometric method" for RUM-based discrete choice.

## Relevance to book chapters

- **Choice framework (ch. 1):** RUM foundations, conditional indirect utility, budget/time constraints, no-ties conditions (pp. 448–450).
- **Drawing densities (ch. 2):** regular canonical representations — transforming uniform draws into draws from target distributions (Gaussian field via Λ(t)Φ^{-1}(ε), Lemma 2 inverse-CDF construction) (pp. 449, 463–464).
- **Simulating data (ch. 3):** the Monte Carlo experiment designs for the specification tests (p. 457) are compact templates for generating discrete choice data with and without taste heterogeneity.
- **MLE (ch. 4) / Optimization (ch. 5):** MSLE as ML with a simulated objective; scaling/parameterization issues (√λ trick, identically-zero scores) relevant to optimization practice (pp. 454, 466).
- **Simulation-assisted estimation (ch. 12):** the core reference — unbiased frequency simulators, importance-sampling derivative simulators, stochastic equicontinuity/fixed draws, r·N^{-1/2} → ∞, Halton draws, MSM vs. MSLE efficiency/robustness tradeoffs, sandwich covariance (pp. 453–456).
- **MNP (ch. 9):** MMNL as a practical approximation to MNP — multivariate normal mixing "can be interpreted as the method for simulation of the MNP model proposed by Stern (1994)"; Monte Carlo evidence that MMNL approximations are as accurate and quick as GHK (pp. 451–452).
- **Latent class (ch. 13):** finite-support G ⇒ latent class; latent-class MMNL as semiparametric treatment of dynamic heterogeneity (Heckman–Singer); sieves + latent class as a fully nonparametric RUM approach (pp. 447, 451–452).
- **Mixed logit (ch. 14):** the foundational paper — definition, approximation theorem, α = β + Λζ factor parameterization, normal/lognormal mixing, error components via alternative-specific dummies, specification tests, and the vehicle-choice application.
- **Bayes-MCMC (ch. 8):** indirectly — the MSLE/MSM machinery here is the classical counterpart students will compare with hierarchical Bayes estimation of the same model (cf. Train's HB vs. MSL comparison paper).
- **Nested/GEV (chs. 10–11):** Dagsvik GEV approximation result and the Laplace-transform characterization of additive EV1 components (pp. 452–453, Lemma 4).
